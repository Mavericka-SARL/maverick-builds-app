AI Developer

Sidebar: Developer › AI Developer

A chat assistant that can read the model and, when you approve, build, change and remove what you build on the other Developer screens, under the same checks. Model changes happen in an isolated AI draft revision that you promote or discard, so the live model is never touched by accident. Some changes are live as soon as you execute the plan, as they are on their own screens: business roles, user access rules, form records posted through a form integration, sign-in connections, whatever a run or a test sends to an external system, and a workflow a fired trigger starts.

It is kept with the plan, carried into every later session and shown in every turn with how much of it the model holds. Activity panelThis session's proposal history.
ProposalsEvery plan is tried against the model before you see it; a plan that would fail goes back to the assistant to correct, so what you confirm is expected to run. It may correct a plan three times, and more (up to eight) while each attempt fails fewer steps; then it proposes the steps that pass and the chat lists the ones the check left out, and after that it stops and says what fails — it is told never to end a turn asking whether to propose. A plan that runs but has a formula that looks like a typical mistake — a Percentage metric multiplied in without dividing by 100, or SUMIFS(src, Region, Region) on a cell that carries every dimension of src (it is the cell's own value; the total is a LOOKUP of the other dimension's total member) — goes back once with a warning; proposed again unchanged, it is shown with the warning above it ("The plan check warns: …"). Dashboards are checked the same way: a new dashboard no role may open, a grid whose metrics have no label or show rates as fractions, a text rewritten whole when a few lines were to change, one grid shown twice on a dashboard with the same rows, columns and scale (one widget shows them together), and, in a model of 30 metrics or more, a metric or grid created without tags. If a step still fails when confirmed, the steps after it are not run (they usually depend on it), and the summary says so. The server also edits a plan itself where the fix is certain, and says so beside it: a layout step that fails (an order, tags) is left out; a metric the plan creates on no grid, read by a metric on a grid, is placed on that grid; when a plan keeps failing, the server proposes the steps that pass and names the rest; and, for a direct order, a formula rewrite, a delete or a value write the order did not ask for is left out. It refuses a saved loader that would drop data rows by a value (an account, a member) unless your message names that value, a formula rewrite that drops everything the formula read when you asked to add to it, and an import of another file when the one your message names is not attached. It warns of a formula reading a dimension property named like an input (the scenario's own values never read), a total over a dimension that totals by none (never computed), a workflow condition reading a metric summed over scenarios, a scenario dimension that adds its members up, and a table's approval workflow without that table as its subject (refused). After a plan is confirmed, the next turn starts with the server's check of its results: metrics with no values at all, calculations that fail, an IFERROR returning its fallback in every cell, and — with a workbook attached — every mapped row of the sheets the plan touched compared again. A provider call that sends nothing for 5 minutes (AI_CALL_IDLE_TIMEOUT), runs past 6 minutes in all (AI_CALL_MAX_DURATION) or loses its connection is asked once more.
Converted filesSpreadsheets the assistant converted to the import layout, and files it wrote for you (a load file, a template, sample data), each with CSV and Excel download buttons. Nothing in them has been imported; ask it to import one, or attach it to the import wizard.

Orders, goals and plans

Each message is read before the assistant works on it, and the status line says how: as a direct order (it names what to make or change — "create dimension Region with members North and South", "rename metric rev to revenue"), as a delegated goal (it leaves the how to the assistant — "build a model from the attached workbook", "make the P&L match the sheet", "add the dashboards people need") or as a question. A direct order is done exactly as written, in one plan: a plan that leaves part of it out, or does something it did not ask for, goes back to the assistant before you see it. A delegated goal is the assistant's to decide within: it follows the instructions for the kind of task (a model from a workbook, numbers that must match a workbook, dashboards, tables and postings, workflows, roles), and a goal that takes more than one plan is planned first: the Plan card above the conversation shows its stages, which one is current and what it delivers. The assistant then proposes one stage at a time, checks each once you confirm it (comparing the workbook's rows, reading the dashboards back) and marks it done; "Confirmed. Continue." goes on with the next stage. A question is answered without a plan. A stage of the plan is marked done when a confirmed plan did every one of its items (the coverage check says so) and every step ran; the assistant marks the rest. A new session takes over the earlier session's plan and decisions, and on its first turn checks the plan's open stages against the model: what the model already holds is marked, not built again. A new goal given while a plan is under way adds its stages after the plan's own; a direct order is done first, whatever stage is current. A question that only asks leave to go on ("shall I continue?") is answered by the server — your request is the leave — and the assistant goes on.

Building from a workbook. Asked to build a model from an attached workbook, the assistant reads the whole workbook first (every sheet's role, layout, input and formula rows, and the order the sheets read each other), agrees the target model with you (it asks the open decisions once), then plans from it: one coherent module per stage in dependency order (for example Hiring Plan, Headcount and payroll together), each loading its inputs and translating and proving its calculations; then the actuals; then the business requirements, once the calculations they read reconcile; the verification checks last. Routine work is the server's: input sheets are loaded by the server, which matches each row to its metric and each column to its member (scenarios, months) -- their numbers are never retyped, and a plan typing values your workbook holds is flagged; a calculation sheet's formulas are translated by the server from the target model, reading every period, so a formula that changes later in the horizon (a rule from month 13, a trailing window over the opening months) becomes a rule by month or is named as a problem; the assistant writes only the rows the rules cannot translate. A stage is done when its rows match the workbook over every period, and it ends with its evidence: what was built, the rows and periods compared (counted by the server), what still differs and why, and the next stage. The plan card shows each stage's evidence and what it took -- minutes, calls, tokens and your corrections -- the decision waiting for you, and how many verification checks are recorded; the build ends when every check is. A difference is reported with the metric, month, scenario, expected and actual value, and fixed at its cause: an input is never moved to make a calculated row match. Every import step on a plan card says where its file comes from -- your file, a sheet of it read by the server, or a file the assistant wrote, which is never imported as your data while your workbook is attached.

How to work with it. Building one three-sheet workbook five ways, a strong reasoning model at low effort (gpt-6.1-sol) finished correctly in 75 calls and about 4 minutes with no questions; at medium effort it took 129 calls; gpt-5-mini did not finish in up to 181 calls, asked what the workbook already said and typed inputs instead of loading them. So: choose a strong model at low effort in AI Settings; attach the workbook; send one short instruction saying what the model is, which sheets are inputs and which calculations, the dimensions to use, and the business requirements and dashboards you want; confirm each plan, and answer the decisions it asks. For a large workbook agree a call budget with a checkpoint, and judge a stage by its evidence, not by the reply. An example instruction: "Build a model from the attached workbook Simple_PnL_Model.xlsx: a monthly P&L for 2027 in EUR. Reproduce its numbers: Assumptions are the inputs, Revenue and P&L are calculations. Use Product and Department as dimensions instead of one line each, and add one dashboard that shows the P&L and a revenue chart. I will confirm each plan."

What it can do

  • Read tools: describe metrics, dimensions, grids (with display levels), dashboard folders, dashboards and each widget, revisions, workflows, triggers, tables of records and their integrations, the records saved in a table (read-only, query_records: a page of records or totals), business roles and users; validate formulas and workflows; answer questions about the model and attached documents.
  • Write tools, always through an approved plan:
    • metrics: create, update, delete;
    • dimensions: create, change (update_dimension: name, tags, parent dimension, property grouping, deriving its members) and delete; members: add, change (code, label, parent, period dates, properties), delete, and generate a run of periods; typed member properties: declare, rename, retype, delete;
    • grids: create, rename, delete, add and remove metrics and dimensions, set a dimension's display level;
    • dashboards: folders, dashboards and widgets — create, change (a widget's place, size, title, what it shows) and delete;
    • tags, revisions, and user access rules on members and on metrics, in the model's active revision only (a business-admin capability given to the assistant on purpose);
    • workflows, triggers, business roles, tables of records and their integrations (below);
    • data: import an attached spreadsheet into a grid (metric values), a dimension (members) or a table (one record per row, posted through the table's mappings), first as a dry run that names every row that would fail, then all-or-nothing into the draft; fill a grid with sample values or clear its values, all of them or under members you name (as Grids › Values does), or hand over the sample rows as a file; write a CSV into the chat for you to download; save the mapping as a re-runnable Excel/CSV integration; create, change and delete data exports (see Integrations › Data Export). Exports created in a session are listed above the message box with a Download button.
    • integrations, as you build and run them in the Integrations tab: REST API connectors — an HTTPS API, an SFTP file, or a model link reading another model of the tenant — with their sign-in connections; Google Sheets integrations (it fetches a sheet you name, as the Sheets import does, previews it, and saves the integration); testing a connector, activating it, scheduling it and Run now; and firing an automation rule's manual trigger. A test without side effects (a GET request, an SFTP file, a model link) runs as soon as the assistant asks, writes nothing and shows it the first records; a test that may change the external system, a run and a trigger are plan steps you confirm. It does each through your own routes, as you: what you may not do, it may not do either — a model link, for instance, only for a developer of both models.
    Each change is the screen's own action with the screen's checks: a dimension or member a formula still uses is refused, a new member code carries its stored values over, a leaf that gains its first child hands its values down to it.
  • Explaining: asked how something works, it looks it up in this manual and the Formulas manual, and in the model itself (its metrics and formulas, grids, dashboards — each dashboard's text, its links and what every widget shows — workflows, triggers and integrations), and says where in the console it is.
  • Asking: whatever only you know — an API's address, where its records sit, an SFTP server and file, which model and grid to read, what a field goes into — it asks for in the chat. It never asks for a password, key or token in the chat: those go into the plan card's sign-in fields. If you paste one into the chat anyway, it will not repeat it; rotate it, because the chat keeps it.
  • The limits of the tenant's edition apply to the assistant as they apply to you.
  • Plans are capped at 100 steps; long tasks are continued in a follow-up plan.
  • What stays yours: test-running a workflow (the assistant proposes publishing it, and your confirmation publishes it), promoting or discarding the draft and deleting revisions, who is in a business role, users, connecting an OAuth authorization-code connection (its Connect click), trusting an SFTP server's host key until you have compared its fingerprint, typing business values and form records (the assistant imports a spreadsheet you attach to its chat, and writes single input values such as a setting with write_input_values, into its draft), and creating applications. Database tables are migrated automatically; there is nothing to run.
Workflows and tables of records. The assistant builds the whole chain a workflow needs: the business roles its steps are assigned to, the definition with every step type the editor offers, the automation rule that starts it, and the form and form integration that feed it. It also publishes (with the checks of the editor's Publish, when you confirm), archives, restores and duplicates workflows, renames and deletes roles and sets the dashboards they may open, and backfills a form integration. It runs the same Validate check as the Workflows editor and reports the verdict with every change. Adding members to a role stays yours; a workflow it does not propose to publish stays a draft, and it tells you so. The assistant uses the same validation as the screens, so a formula or a workflow it proposes is rejected under the same rules you would be.

Roles

Sidebar: Developer › Roles (hidden when you also hold business_admin: the Business Admin console's Roles tab is the same screen)

Named business roles ("Budget Approvers", "Regional Managers"). Workflow tasks and approvals are assigned to these roles by name, or to the tenant roles. A developer creates the roles their workflows need, decides which dashboards each may open, and may add and remove its members, as a business admin does.

Where the rest of access is configured

Three things decide what a user sees and may change. Role membership and access rules live in the Business Admin console (sidebar group Business Admin), which a developer sees only when they also hold the business_admin role or ask a business admin:

  • Roles. The roles above, with their members.
  • Dashboard access. Per role, the dashboards its members may open (Save dashboard access). A user who holds any business role sees only the dashboards granted to their roles; one who holds none sees every dashboard.
  • Access rules. Per user, an Access level of write, read or hidden for individual dimension members and metrics (Save rules). Hidden members disappear from grids, charts and selectors; read makes cells read-only; rules on a parent member cascade to its descendants. A hidden metric hides every calculated metric built on it too, directly or through others, and a formula's text is shown only to the model's developers.
